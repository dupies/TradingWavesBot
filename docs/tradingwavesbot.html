01 The trade, candle by candle

What the bot is actually looking for

The bot watches one 1-hour candle a day and then waits. At 08:00 London it records the high and the low of that hour — the opening range. Everything after that is a reaction to that range.

From 09:00 onward it checks each closed 1H candle. If a candle closes above the range high, it buys. If one closes below the range low, it sells. Closes inside the range don't count; after three such candles the setup is abandoned and the bot does nothing more with that symbol until tomorrow. One trade per symbol per day, maximum.

Note the word closes. A wick poking through the range does not trigger anything — the bot only ever acts on finished candles, and it only enters at the open of the candle after the one that triggered it. That one-candle lag is deliberate: a decision made on a closed candle cannot honestly be executed inside it, and a backtest that pretends otherwise is lying to you.

Where the stop and targets come from

The stop goes on the far side of the range — the range low for a buy, the range high for a sell — pushed out by a 2-pip buffer so that spread alone doesn't take you out. In the picture above that's a 28-pip stop.

That stop distance is what sets your position size, not the other way around. The bot risks 1% of equity on the trade and solves for lots:

lots = (equity × 1%) ÷ (stop_pips × pip_value_per_lot)

on $10,000 equity with a 28-pip stop on EURUSD:
        100 ÷ (28 × 10.00) = 0.357 → rounded down to 0.35 lots

Rounding is always down to the broker's lot step. Rounding up would quietly risk more than the 1% you asked for, and a bot that silently exceeds its own risk setting is worse than no bot.

The ladder, and why it sometimes gets shorter

The position exits in four 25% slices, one at each target. When TP1 fills, the stop moves to your entry price — from that point the trade cannot lose money, barring a gap or slippage through the stop.

A four-rung ladder needs at least four minimum lots to execute, because your broker won't accept a 0.0025-lot partial close. So a 0.03-lot position physically cannot be closed in four pieces. Rather than entering a trade whose later exits would be rejected, the bot shortens the ladder to what the size can honour — keeping the nearest targets and dropping the furthest. A 0.03-lot position runs TP1, TP2 and TP3 at 0.01 each. If the size can't honour even one rung, the trade isn't taken.

02 The guards

Nine ways a setup gets refused — each one logged

Most of what this bot does is decline to trade. Every refusal is written to the journal with a machine-readable code and a sentence of explanation, so you can always answer the question "why didn't it take that?" after the fact.

There are two layers. The strategy vetoes setups that don't look like its setup. The risk engine vetoes trades the account can't afford — and it sits on the single path every order takes, which means a signal you send in by hand later (Phase 4) is subject to exactly the same caps.

How exposure is measured

Not notional value. The risk engine adds up what you lose if every open stop is hit and compares that to 10% of equity. On a leveraged account notional value is a meaningless number; money at stake is the one that can actually bankrupt you. A position whose stop has moved to breakeven contributes zero.

03 How it's put together

Why the backtest and the live bot are the same code

The bot is built as a plain Python core — price maths, strategy rules, risk limits, the journal — with no knowledge whatsoever of MetaTrader. Around that core sit interchangeable adapters. Nothing in core/ imports a broker, touches the network, or reads the clock; time arrives as a parameter.

That's not architectural tidiness for its own sake. It buys two specific things: the whole test suite runs on your Mac with no MT5 installed, and the code that places real orders is the same code the backtest exercised.

One interface, three venues

Backtesting, paper trading and live trading differ only in which adapter is loaded. That is one line in config/settings.yaml.

04 The journal

Your audit trail, and the reason a restart isn't a disaster

Every signal, every sizing decision, every veto and every fill lands in a SQLite file with four tables: signals, decisions, positions, fills. It's a plain file you can open with any SQLite browser.

It does two jobs. First, it answers "why" — including for trades that never happened, which is the harder and more useful question. Second, it's what the bot reads on startup to rebuild its own state.

Restarting mid-trade

The broker is the source of truth; the bot's memory is only a cache. On startup the engine asks the broker what's open, matches each position against the journal by its magic number (770001), and rebuilds from there. A restart with TP1 and TP2 filled and half the position still running resumes with the correct remaining volume and stop placement.

Positions it finds with a different magic number — your own manual trades in the same account — are logged and left strictly alone. Positions with no journal record are also left alone. The bot never manages an order it can't account for.

One thing worth understanding about the engine loop: when a stop or a target fills, that happens inside the broker, not through a call the bot makes. So on every candle the engine first drains the fills that occurred since last time, journals them, and adds them to the running daily total. Without that step the daily loss cap would never see a loss and your headline safety control would do nothing at all. That was a real bug found and fixed during Phase 1.

05 "Training" the bot

There is no model — and that's the honest answer

Set expectations here

This bot does not learn, and there is no neural network inside it to train. It is a rule-based system: nine or ten numbers in a config file drive a fixed set of if-statements. Machine-learning trade filtering was considered during design and deliberately deferred.

What "training" means here is parameter optimization — searching for the numbers that held up on history — followed by validation designed to catch you fooling yourself. That's Phase 2, and it runs entirely on your Mac with no broker account.

Step one: the sweep

Take the parameters that are genuinely uncertain and try combinations of them over real history, using the same sim_broker the backtest already uses:

ParameterTodayWorth sweeping because
tp_pips100/200/300/400The prime suspect. These are 5–20× the range that generated the signal

sl_buffer_pips2Too tight and spread stops you out; too wide and it costs size

Step two: walk-forward, or the sweep means nothing

A grid search over a few years of data will always find a combination that looks brilliant. It found the noise. The only question that matters is whether parameters chosen on one slice of history survive on a slice they never saw.

What you need before any of this is worth running

Real EURUSD H1 history — several years of it, exported from MT5 or any data vendor, as time,open,high,low,close,volume. The current data/EURUSD_H1_synthetic.csv is a generated random walk. It proves the plumbing works. It tells you nothing about the strategy.

Two commands, no broker account, no MT5

Three things in that table you should not skim past

A profit factor of 1.25 on a random walk is noise. It demonstrates that candles flow through the strategy, the risk engine, the broker and the metrics without crashing. It is not a result. Don't let it become one in your memory.

"Win rate" counts exit fills, not positions. A trade that hits TP1 and TP2 then stops out at breakeven contributes two wins and one neutral exit — from one position. Per position, only 15 of 68 reached any target at all. That is the honest figure, and the metrics module should be reporting it.

MAX_POSITIONS caused 15 of the 22 skips. This is a genuine design collision, not a tuning detail: targets 100–400 pips away mean positions are held for days, positions held for days accumulate, and three is the ceiling. The bot spent much of the run full and refusing new setups. Either the targets shrink or the position limit rises — the Phase 2 sweep should decide which, and it is the first thing the optimizer will argue with.

Where the simulator is deliberately unkind to itself

  • If a candle touches both the stop and a target, the stop wins. Tick order inside a candle is unknowable, and flattering a backtest is how strategies look profitable right up until they trade real money.
  • Entries fill at the next candle's open, never inside the signal candle.
  • Spread is charged as a half-spread on both entry and exit.
A gap that is real but not a bug

Position size is calculated from the signal candle's close, but the fill happens at the next candle's open. When the market gaps, your realised risk differs from your intended 1%. This is inherent to market entries rather than a defect — but it's real, and on a gappy open a "1% trade" can be a 1.4% trade. Worth knowing before you size up.

07 Getting to a live order

Five gates, in order, none skippable

Two infrastructure facts shape all of this. The MetaTrader5 Python package is Windows-only, so live and paper trading need a Windows VPS — your Mac can do everything up to that point but cannot place an order. And MT5 keeps its own server time, which is usually not London time; the session hour gets translated at runtime, and getting that translation wrong means marking the wrong candle as the opening range.

1

Backtest on real history

Replace the synthetic CSV with several years of actual EURUSD H1 candles and run it. Read the rejection list before the metrics.

it trades at a plausible frequency and the drawdown is survivable on your account size
2

Optimize, then validate out-of-sample

Phase 2: the sweep plus walk-forward from section 05. Expect the 100/200/300/400 targets to change.

out-of-sample results are in the same league as in-sample — not merely positive
3

Paper trade on live prices

Phase 3: paper_broker on the VPS. Real quotes, real spread, real session times, no orders. This is where timezone and spread assumptions get found out.

paper entries land where the backtest said they would, on the candles it said they would
4

Demo account

Real MT5 order flow against a demo balance. Now you're testing execution: slippage, rejected partial closes, stop modifications, weekend gaps, and a deliberate mid-trade restart to prove reconciliation works.

the journal matches the MT5 history, trade for trade, including a restart
5

Live, at a fraction of the size

Drop risk_per_trade_pct to something that cannot hurt — 0.1 or 0.25 — and leave it there for a meaningful number of trades. Your real enemies here are not strategy quality but a VPS reboot, a dropped MT5 connection, and a broker whose contract specs differ from symbols.yaml.

live fills track demo fills, and nothing operational has surprised you
Check this against your broker before gate 4

config/symbols.yaml currently assumes pip_value_per_lot: 10.00 for USD-quoted pairs and 6.70 for USDJPY. The USD-quoted figure is reliable on a USD account; the USDJPY one varies with price and is an approximation. Every lot size the bot calculates depends on these numbers, so confirm them against your broker's contract specification. A wrong pip value means every position is the wrong size, silently, in a way no test can catch.

08 What a trading day looks like

Once it's running on the VPS

The bot wakes on each closed 1H candle and does three things in a fixed order: collect any fills the broker produced on its own, manage what's already open, then look for a new entry. That order matters — a stop that filled overnight has to be counted against the daily loss cap before the cap is consulted on a new trade.

TimeWhat the bot doesWhat you see
08:00Marks the high and low of the opening hourNothing — no order, no alert
09:00First check for a close outside the rangeA journal row either way
09:00–11:00Up to three candles to break out, then the setup is abandoned for the dayA veto with its reason, if it's abandoned
on breakoutSizes to 1%, checks four caps, builds the ladder, enters at the next openPosition opens; Phase 5 adds a Telegram alert
hourly afterDrains fills, rolls the daily tally, moves the stop to breakeven once TP1 fillsPartial closes appear in MT5
next dayDaily loss tally resets; a new range is markedYesterday's trade, if still open, keeps being managed
 Trades routinely survive past the day they opened — that's a consequence of 100–400 pip targets on a 1H signal, not a malfunction.

Three things to watch, in order of how much they'd cost you

  1. MAX_POSITIONS in the rejection log. If the bot is constantly full, you're not trading the strategy you think you are — you're trading whichever setups happened to arrive when a slot was free.
  2. The journal versus MT5 history. They should agree trade for trade. If they drift, stop and find out why before adding size.
  3. Drawdown, not profit. A profit factor above 1 is worth nothing if the path there is deeper than your account — or your nerve — can survive. On the test run that path was 20.5% deep.
